Continuity

Mathematical background and its role in the reconstruction problem.

General

Continuity means that sufficiently small changes to an input produce small changes to an output, using the chosen meanings of “small.” It does not always tell us an explicit error schedule.

Example and distinction

A detector may respond gently to small changes in a wave measured in one norm. Changing how wave size is measured can change whether that statement holds. An explicit bound makes the dependence quantitatively usable.

Physics

Continuity expresses stability of a map under the topology chosen for its inputs and outputs. For a detector functional, it connects control of field approximations to control of measured values.

Use in the model

A statement of continuity and a supplied estimate are different data. The wave reconstruction uses explicit bounds where needed; the rate-free amplitude problem cannot be solved merely by naming the detector continuous.

Mathematics

A map is continuous when inverse images of open sets are open; metric formulations use ε–δ conditions. Uniform continuity and continuity at individual points are distinct assertions.

Conditions and consequences

A modulus records a quantitative dependency between tolerances. Neither its existence in a chosen formal system nor its availability as an input follows just by writing a pointwise continuity hypothesis.

Specialist

Continuity must be indexed by the actual carrier and target representation. The detector estimates in the benchmark do not supply missing tail information for a rate-free input.

Project role and claim boundary

When composing constructions, verify the topology and quantitative resources required at each interface. A continuous abstract map is not automatically a computable map between the declared codes, nor a uniform reconstruction operator.
