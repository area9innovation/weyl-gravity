Operator and domain

Mathematical structures, hypotheses and their role in the atlas.

General

An operator is a rule acting on mathematical objects. Its domain says which inputs the rule is allowed to act on. A formula without its domain may leave the problem ambiguous.

Example and distinction

Differentiating a smooth function is straightforward; differentiating an arbitrary rough function needs a different meaning. Boundary conditions can also change the operator, even when the displayed differential formula stays the same.

Physics

The domain is part of an operator’s physical specification, particularly for unbounded observables and generators. Boundary conditions and regularity can determine its spectrum and evolution.

Use in the model

A formal Hamiltonian expression does not establish self-adjointness or a unitary evolution. Likewise, a mode formula for an inverse does not fix the source and target spaces required of a causal Green operator.

Mathematics

An operator is a map D(A)→Y with declared domain D(A), often a proper dense subspace of a Hilbert or Banach space. Closure and extension properties depend on that domain.

Conditions and consequences

Two operators can share a differential expression yet have different domains and adjoints. Formal integration by parts checks an expression-level relation, not equality with the Hilbert-space adjoint on its full domain.

Specialist

Domain compatibility is an interface obligation when passing from finite exact data to analytic carriers. Record the actual spaces, boundary conditions and extension used.

Project role and claim boundary

Neither a chain-map certificate nor a real finite spectrum proves the needed unbounded-operator statements. Importing a spectral calculation without its domain can change the claim being transported.
