Weak solution

Mathematical structures, hypotheses and their role in the atlas.

General

A weak solution satisfies an equation through its responses to test functions rather than through ordinary derivatives at every point. It allows useful solutions that are too rough for the pointwise formulation.

Example and distinction

Instead of asking for a field’s value at every tiny location, one checks suitable averaged identities. This broadens the allowed solutions but does not remove the need for initial data, boundary conditions or a uniqueness argument.

Physics

A weak formulation moves derivatives onto tests or uses a variational identity on declared spaces. It can express dynamics for finite-energy or distributional fields.

Use in the model

The precise testing class determines the assertion. Satisfying that identity does not itself establish causal support, smoothness or the full gauge constraints; those remain separate conditions on the proposed physical solution.

Mathematics

A weak solution satisfies a specified integral or dual identity for all admissible tests. Distributional and variational formulations can be related, but equivalence depends on spaces and hypotheses.

Conditions and consequences

Regularity theorems may upgrade a weak solution to a classical one. That upgrade is not part of the definition, and an exact finite residual check covers only the declared ansatz unless a full testing argument is supplied.

Specialist

Keep the ladder’s weak-equation statement tied to its test class and carrier. Extending finite fixtures to all tests requires the relevant continuity or density argument.

Project role and claim boundary

A weak scalar equation does not supply the full metric complex’s causal inverse or physical state. Imported distributional claims must retain their operator, domain, support and regularity boundaries.
