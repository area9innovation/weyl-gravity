Reverse implication

Mathematical background and its role in the reconstruction problem.

General

A reverse implication shows that being able to solve the proposed problem would let us recover an assumption used to solve it. It is the direction that explains why an assumption is necessary.

Example and distinction

Showing that a stronger rulebook can solve a problem proves sufficiency. Showing that any solution method for every allowed input yields that rulebook’s extra existence principle gives the converse comparison. Both directions have to use the stated common starting rules.

Physics

A reversal reduces a mathematical existence principle to the target reconstruction task. It identifies an unavoidable requirement of the stated task, rather than a failure of one numerical algorithm.

Use in the model

For the wave benchmark, a scalar encoding can be recovered through a fixed detector at time zero. The lower bound therefore does not depend on difficult propagation or varying the physical apparatus across the reduction.

Mathematics

Over a base B, the reverse implication T implies A shows that the target assertion T entails the principle A. Together with A implying T, it gives the stated equivalence.

Conditions and consequences

The input encoding must be available in B, and the output must support the required decoding. An informal identification of limiting objects is insufficient if the theorem demands a particular name for that object.

Specialist

The WR reversal uses scalar amplitude coding to recover range existence, hence ACA₀ over RCA₀. The fixed profile and detector locate the cost in completion rather than evolution.

Project role and claim boundary

The classical scalar reversal is standard background; the project’s contribution is its realization in the declared wave-observable benchmark. Exact finite checks support the encoding but do not replace the written infinite proof.
