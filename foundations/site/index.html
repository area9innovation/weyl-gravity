An equation is a beginning

Suppose someone hands you an elegant equation and says it could describe the universe. Before believing them, you would want to know what it describes, how things change, what can be measured, and whether the predictions agree with observations. An equation alone cannot answer all of those questions.

This programme began with an unusual theory of gravity called Weyl gravity. It grew into a broader investigation: start with the jobs a physical theory must do, then identify which physical ideas and mathematical assumptions make each job possible. We call this reverse foundations of physics. The aim is a clearer route from an interesting idea to a testable claim.

From equations to physical obligations

The programme combines two questions: whether pure Weyl gravity and related fourth-order models survive their classical, quantum and empirical tests, and which assumptions each test actually uses. The unit of progress is an obligation—such as state existence, causal propagation or a calibrated prediction—rather than a theory name.

The two introductory papers motivate the same decomposition. Physical postulates, mathematical realizations and logical existence principles are distinct inputs. A result about one realization must not acquire a stronger interpretation merely because the notation resembles that of another. This introduction presents that programme, with later corrections incorporated below.

What structures make a prediction possible?

A physical model is more than a differential equation. It needs a space of admissible data, evolution, quantities extracted from solutions, and a bridge to observations. Existence, uniqueness, continuity and probability can depend on different structures and hypotheses.

The project studies that dependency explicitly. Reverse foundations of physics is broader than reverse mathematics: it compares physical premises and mathematical representations as well as logical strength. Its origin in pure Weyl gravity provides concrete problems; the general question is which assumptions suffice for each specified task and which can be proved necessary.

The assumptions beneath the answer

A theory begins with physical ideas: how systems affect one another, which changes leave the laws unchanged, and what can count as a measurement. It also chooses a mathematical setting. A finite table of numbers and a space containing every possible wave are different settings, even if one approximates the other.

A further layer concerns what mathematics allows us to assume exists. For instance, better and better finite approximations need a justified connection to a limiting object. Finally, the model needs rules for predictions and probabilities, and a fair comparison with data. Changing any of these layers can change the conclusion.

Keep five inputs visible

Separate physical postulates, the carrier used to realize them, logic and existence assumptions, operational identifications, and empirical protocols. Locality or conformal symmetry is a physical premise; a Hilbert completion or an operator domain is mathematical structure; a detector functional and a noise model add different operational information.

The distinction matters at familiar transitions: from finite truncations to continuum fields, from Euclidean constructions to Lorentzian propagation, and from an indefinite gauge description to physical probabilities. Each transition needs a theorem or an explicitly stated premise. A successful calculation on one side does not supply that bridge by itself.

The ambient object is part of the theorem

A carrier includes the relevant topology, pairing and operator domains, not merely an underlying set or vector space. A change of topology can change which operations are continuous; a change of pairing can change positivity. A representation of input data can include or omit a convergence rate.

Logical assumptions form another layer. A proof may assert that an infinite set or limit exists without supplying an effective construction. Reverse mathematics studies the strength of such assertions over a fixed base. The wider programme also asks whether another carrier preserves the intended observable while avoiding a particular assumption.

Why the distinctions matter

The gravity studies ask several different questions about extra solutions: do they exist mathematically, survive the rules that remove redundant descriptions, remain possible when interactions are included, and support sensible probabilities? The word ghost is often used around these questions, but one answer cannot settle all of them.

Other examples test different connections. Mannheim’s proposal uses conformal gravity to explain how galaxies rotate; comparing its predictions with data requires stating how measurement errors are weighted. A curve can do better by one score and worse by another. A stability shortcut for Bateman and Turok’s proposal for higher-derivative physics can fail without disproving their entire theory. Each case needs a precise question and a conclusion with explicit limits.

Different mathematical failures have different force

A local solution may fail a global compatibility condition. A nontrivial cohomology class may have no demonstrated positive state interpretation. A family of finite constructions may lack a uniform estimate needed to pass to a limit. These are different obstructions and should have different conclusions.

The introductory papers illustrate this with fourth-order gravity, a Euclidean stability counterfamily, and bounded empirical comparisons. The counterexample to a sufficient estimate does not negate every possible construction; a comparison of fitted residuals does not identify a model without its statistical and physical assumptions.

How to judge AI-assisted research

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems help this programme derive equations, write software, challenge arguments and draft explanations. They can also produce convincing mistakes. Clear writing matters because a reader must be able to see what was assumed, what was checked and where the argument could fail.

The evidence trail therefore links a claim to its calculation and checks. Repeating the same calculation is useful, but it is not the same as checking it independently. A computer proof establishes the statement it was given; it cannot decide whether that statement models nature. Asger Alstrup Palm is the accountable human contact. Cauchy sequence

Continuity
