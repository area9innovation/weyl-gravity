An equation is a beginning

Suppose someone hands you an elegant equation and says it could describe the universe. Before believing them, you would want to know what it describes, how things change, what can be measured, and whether the predictions agree with observations. An equation alone cannot answer all of those questions.

This programme began with an unusual theory of gravity called Weyl gravity. It grew into a broader investigation: start with the jobs a physical theory must do, then identify which physical ideas and mathematical assumptions make each job possible. We call this reverse foundations of physics. The aim is a clearer route from an interesting idea to a testable claim.

From equations to physical obligations

The programme combines two questions: whether pure Weyl gravity and related fourth-order models survive their classical, quantum and empirical tests, and which assumptions each test actually uses. The unit of progress is an obligation—such as state existence, causal propagation or a calibrated prediction—rather than a theory name.

The two introductory papers motivate the same decomposition. Physical postulates, mathematical realizations and logical existence principles are distinct inputs. A result about one realization must not acquire a stronger interpretation merely because the notation resembles that of another. This introduction presents that programme, with later corrections incorporated below.

What structures make a prediction possible?

A physical model is more than a differential equation. It needs a space of admissible data, evolution, quantities extracted from solutions, and a bridge to observations. Existence, uniqueness, continuity and probability can depend on different structures and hypotheses.

The project studies that dependency explicitly. Reverse foundations of physics is broader than reverse mathematics: it compares physical premises and mathematical representations as well as logical strength. Its origin in pure Weyl gravity provides concrete problems; the general question is which assumptions suffice for each specified task and which can be proved necessary.

The assumptions beneath the answer

A theory begins with physical ideas: how systems affect one another, which changes leave the laws unchanged, and what can count as a measurement. It also chooses a mathematical setting. A finite table of numbers and a space containing every possible wave are different settings, even if one approximates the other.

A further layer concerns what mathematics allows us to assume exists. For instance, better and better finite approximations need a justified connection to a limiting object. Finally, the model needs rules for predictions and probabilities, and a fair comparison with data. Changing any of these layers can change the conclusion.

Keep five inputs visible

Separate physical postulates, the carrier used to realize them, logic and existence assumptions, operational identifications, and empirical protocols. Locality or conformal symmetry is a physical premise; a Hilbert completion or an operator domain is mathematical structure; a detector functional and a noise model add different operational information.

The distinction matters at familiar transitions: from finite truncations to continuum fields, from Euclidean constructions to Lorentzian propagation, and from an indefinite gauge description to physical probabilities. Each transition needs a theorem or an explicitly stated premise. A successful calculation on one side does not supply that bridge by itself.

The ambient object is part of the theorem

A carrier includes the relevant topology, pairing and operator domains, not merely an underlying set or vector space. A change of topology can change which operations are continuous; a change of pairing can change positivity. A representation of input data can include or omit a convergence rate.

Logical assumptions form another layer. A proof may assert that an infinite set or limit exists without supplying an effective construction. Reverse mathematics studies the strength of such assertions over a fixed base. The wider programme also asks whether another carrier preserves the intended observable while avoiding a particular assumption.

Type the interfaces, not only the endpoints

Record which structures a comparison preserves: differential, pairing, causal support, observable identification, positivity, normalization or empirical meaning. Why the distinctions matter

The gravity studies ask several different questions about extra solutions: do they exist mathematically, survive the rules that remove redundant descriptions, remain possible when interactions are included, and support sensible probabilities? The word ghost is often used around these questions, but one answer cannot settle all of them.

Other examples test different connections. Mannheim’s proposal uses conformal gravity to explain how galaxies rotate; comparing its predictions with data requires stating how measurement errors are weighted. A curve can do better by one score and worse by another. A stability shortcut for Bateman and Turok’s proposal for higher-derivative physics can fail without disproving their entire theory. Each case needs a precise question and a conclusion with explicit limits.

Different mathematical failures have different force

A local solution may fail a global compatibility condition. A nontrivial cohomology class may have no demonstrated positive state interpretation. A family of finite constructions may lack a uniform estimate needed to pass to a limit. These are different obstructions and should have different conclusions.

The introductory papers illustrate this with fourth-order gravity, a Euclidean stability counterfamily, and bounded empirical comparisons. The counterexample to a sufficient estimate does not negate every possible construction; a comparison of fitted residuals does not identify a model without its statistical and physical assumptions.

How to judge AI-assisted research

Artificial intelligence (AI) systems help this programme derive equations, write software, challenge arguments and draft explanations. They can also produce convincing mistakes. Clear writing matters because a reader must be able to see what was assumed, what was checked and where the argument could fail.

The evidence trail therefore links a claim to its calculation and checks. Repeating the same calculation is useful, but it is not the same as checking it independently. A computer proof establishes the statement it was given; it cannot decide whether that statement models nature. Asger Alstrup Palm is the accountable human contact. The work remains open to criticism on its evidence.

Separate kinds of evidence

An exact algebraic identity, a numerical benchmark, a written infinite-dimensional proof and a proof-assistant derivation answer different questions. An independent checker is useful only if it does not silently inherit the producer’s decisive assumption. A skip or timeout contributes no passing evidence.

The unusual AI-assisted authorship increases the importance of explicit sources and reproducible arguments. The website is a current explanatory account, while papers preserve dated arguments. Neither fluent prose nor a machine-readable certificate supplies missing physical premises or substitutes for independent scientific review.

ACA₀

General ACA stands for Arithmetical Comprehension Axiom. ACA₀ extends the starting rulebook RCA₀ with permission to collect numbers satisfying an arithmetic condition into a set, even when no general decision procedure is supplied.

Physics ACA stands for Arithmetical Comprehension Axiom. ACA₀ adds arithmetic comprehension to the base theory. In this wave problem it supplies missing tail-selection information needed to turn a rate-free Cauchy input into the required named limit.

Mathematics ACA stands for Arithmetical Comprehension Axiom. The extension of RCA₀ by comprehension for arithmetical formulas: number quantifiers are allowed, with set parameters, but no bound set variables. The wave statement WR is equivalent to this extension over RCA₀.

Specialist ACA stands for Arithmetical Comprehension Axiom. The exact strength of WR over RCA₀ for the declared rate-free representation. The upper bound selects Cauchy-tail witnesses; the lower bound recovers range existence through one scalar amplitude at t=0.

Carrier

General The kind of mathematical structure in which a model is built. A finite collection of matrices, a space of waves, and an algebra of quantum quantities are different carriers, with different questions that must be checked.

Physics The specified mathematical realization carrying the model’s variables, operations and states: for example finite matrices, a completed function space or a local algebraic construction. Changing carrier may change which analytic obligations are meaningful.

Mathematics The chosen structured ambient object, including any topology, pairing, operator domains and completion data needed for a result. The matrix uses carrier as an organizational dimension rather than as one universally defined category.

Specialist The typed support of a construction in the atlas. Cross-carrier transfer requires a declared interface preserving the relevant operations and hypotheses; matching labels or finite fixtures do not establish equivalence.

Convergence rate (modulus)

General An error schedule: for each requested accuracy, it tells you how far along an approximation sequence you must go so that all later approximations agree to that accuracy.

Physics A tolerance-to-index function controlling all later differences between input approximants in a specified norm. Supplying this function is stronger input information than promising that the sequence eventually settles.

Mathematics A function μ such that m,n≥μ(k) implies d(pₘ,pₙ)≤2⁻ᵏ. The Cauchy statement asserts existence of a suitable index for each k; a supplied modulus gives those indices as one input function.

Specialist An input function witnessing the ordinary-Cauchy promise in the exact second-order representation. WR omits it; WRμ includes it. This changes the completion obligation while preserving the detector and dynamics.

Observable

General A quantity a model connects to a possible measurement, such as position, energy, or an average wave amplitude. The observable tells us what we are asking about; the state supplies the information used to predict it.

Physics A specified quantity evaluated on a classical state, or represented by an appropriate operator or algebra element in quantum theory. Its mathematical definition and domain must be fixed before discussing predictions.

Mathematics A map extracting a quantity from a state, with the required structure depending on the model. In the wave example it is a bounded linear functional; quantum observables instead belong to an operator or algebraic framework.

Reverse mathematics

General A way of asking which mathematical assumptions a theorem really needs. First show that certain assumptions prove the theorem; then reason backwards from the theorem to see whether it recovers those assumptions.

Physics An analysis of the axiom strength of a mathematical assertion after its inputs, outputs and representations have been fixed. It can separate the cost of taking limits from the physical evolution being modeled.

Mathematics The study of implications and reversals over a specified base theory. An equivalence B⊢(T↔S) calibrates T against the additional principles S, with B held fixed.

Specialist An equivalence or implication analysis whose base, representation and quantifier form are part of the theorem. Here the focal comparison is RCA₀⊢WRμ versus RCA₀⊢(WR↔ACA₀).

State

General The mathematical information a model uses to make predictions about a system. What counts as a state depends on the model: a moving particle may need position and velocity, while a quantum model uses a different kind of description.

Physics A classical phase-space point or field initial datum, or a quantum density operator or normalized positive functional on an observable algebra. The carrier and positivity requirements must be stated; a cohomology class is not automatically a physical state.

Mathematics A model-dependent object on which predictions depend. Examples include a phase-space point and a normalized positive linear functional ω on a unital *-algebra, satisfying ω(A* A)≥0 and ω(1)=1.

Reverse implication

General A reverse implication shows that being able to solve the proposed problem would let us recover an assumption used to solve it. It is the direction that explains why an assumption is necessary.

Cauchy sequence

General A Cauchy sequence is a list whose later entries become arbitrarily close to one another. This describes how the list settles, even before a limiting object has been constructed.

Physics A sequence is Cauchy when its tail differences become arbitrarily small in the declared metric or norm. The relevant notion of error depends on the carrier.

Mathematics In a metric space, a sequence is Cauchy if for every positive ε there is N such that d(x_m,x_n) is below ε whenever m,n are at least N.

Specialist The rate-free input in WR carries a Cauchy promise without the schedule supplied in WRμ. The target still requires an appropriately fast name for the observable.

Fast Cauchy name

General A fast Cauchy name describes a number or other object by approximations with an explicit, rapidly shrinking error rule. It tells a reader how much accuracy later entries guarantee.

Physics A fast name packages approximations with a prescribed accuracy schedule. It makes the detector output usable at requested tolerances without having to infer a convergence rate afterward.

Mathematics A fast Cauchy name is a coded approximation sequence satisfying a fixed effective Cauchy or limit-error bound, typically using powers of two. The exact convention belongs to the representation.

Specialist The requested fast output name enables the decoding used by the WR lower bound. Dropping that output requirement would change the assertion under study.

Completion

General Completion enlarges a space so that its intended Cauchy approximation sequences have limits inside it. It makes precise a step often hidden in the phrase “take finer and finer approximations.”

Physics Completion passes from a preliminary space, such as finite mode combinations, to a space containing limits in a specified norm or topology. It is an additional construction, not a consequence of finite-mode success.

Mathematics A metric completion can be constructed from equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences, with sequences equivalent when their mutual distance tends to zero. The original space embeds densely.

Specialist Completion is a typed transition with both analytic and logical dependencies. The wave comparison isolates amplitude completion; the ladder distinguishes finite algebra, Cauchy, distributional and energy carriers.

Continuity

Supplied-rate and rate-free wave reconstruction

General WR names the wave-reconstruction assertion with a rate-free Cauchy input. WRμ names its supplied-rate version; μ denotes an error schedule. Both ask for a reliably approximated reading of the same fixed wave detector.

Physics The benchmark fixes the wave profile and detector while comparing rate-free Cauchy data with data carrying a modulus. WRμ is provable in RCA₀; WR is equivalent to ACA₀ over that base.

Mathematics The assertions use specified input codings and require a fast output name for the detector observable. Supplying μ allows explicit reconstruction; omitting it permits a reduction from range existence over RCA₀.

Specialist Preserve both directions and the representation boundary: RCA₀ proves WRμ, and over RCA₀ the declared WR is equivalent to ACA₀. No higher-type uniform reconstruction theorem is claimed.
